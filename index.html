Interference Lab

Radio signals share the air. This lab shows three ways they get in each other's way — waves cancelling, noise burying a message, and too many devices crowding one channel. Nothing is transmitted; it's all math on a screen.

Wave A Wave B A + B (what a receiver hears)
Set B to the same frequency and strength as A, then slide the phase to 180°.

What's happening: two waves in the same space add together point by point. Line them up (phase 0°) and they reinforce — the combined wave gets taller. Flip B to 180° at equal frequency and strength and every peak meets a trough, so they cancel to a flat line. That total cancellation is destructive interference — the exact principle a jammer abuses to drown out a real signal.

What's happening: the letter is sent as eight bits — a high level for 1, low for 0. The receiver reads each bit by asking "is it above or below the decision line?" Noise is random wobble added on top. A little is harmless, but as the noise floor rises it buries the signal, pushes bits across the line, and the letter arrives as something else. Real systems fight this with error-correcting codes that add extra bits to catch and repair flips.

One device has the channel to itself.

What's happening: every device shares one channel and tries to talk in short time slots. If exactly one talks in a slot, its message gets through (green). If two or more talk at once they collide and both have to resend (red). Adding devices helps at first, then each new talker causes more collisions than successes and useful throughput collapses. That's why one Wi-Fi access point slows to a crawl when a whole class piles onto it — congestion, not weak signal.
